I didn't expect traffic to be one of the things that surprised me most
about Taiwan, but here we are.

## Cars actually stop for you

There's a moment I keep thinking about. I was crossing a street near
campus, no traffic light in sight, and a car just stopped. Fully stopped,
waited for me to cross, then went on its way. No honking, no creeping
forward, no making me feel like I needed to hurry. Back in Ho Chi Minh
City, crossing without a light is basically an exercise in trust and a bit
of luck. Here it felt like the driver simply assumed I had the right of
way, because I did.

![A car driving down a wide, tree-lined road near Ming Chuan University, with bicycles and benches on the median](/blog/campus-road-benches.jpg)

_A quiet road near campus. Wide lanes, plenty of green, and drivers who
don't seem to be in a rush._

## Where did all the motorbikes go

The other thing that caught me off guard: how few motorbikes there are.
Coming from Ho Chi Minh City, where the streets feel like a constant river
of them, seeing mostly cars and pedestrians instead took some getting used
to. It changes the whole rhythm of a street. Less noise, less weaving,
less of that feeling that you always need to watch your back.

![A wide road near Ming Chuan University with clear lane markings, parked motorbikes off to the side, and trees lining the street](/blog/campus-road-wide-lane.jpg)

_The road right by school. Motorbikes exist, they're just not the main
event._

## The air, too

It's a small thing until you notice it, but the air near campus is just
clean. No haze, no exhaust hanging over the street, nothing catching in
the back of your throat after a walk. Combined with the wide sidewalks
and the drivers who actually stop, it adds up to something that just
feels calmer.

## Getting used to it

I don't think I'll stop noticing this for a while. Every time a car stops
for me without being asked to, it still catches me a little off guard, in
a good way.
